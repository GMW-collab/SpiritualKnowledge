## discover

If one practice stands at the centre of Sikh spiritual life, it is **Naam**: remembering the Divine, keeping the One in mind, until remembrance stops being an activity and becomes the ground of who we are. The first ashtapadi of Sukhmani Sahib opens with it.

[[gurbani:simrau-simar]]

Naam (ਨਾਮ) literally means ‘name’, but in Gurbani it means far more than a word. It is the Divine presence as it can be known and lived: the Order and reality of Waheguru, which the Gurus say pervades everything. *Simran* and *jap* are the ways we turn towards it.

## understand

**Naam is not only words.** Sikh practice includes repeating Gurmantar, reading and singing Gurbani, and keeping Waheguru in mind through the day. Gurbani honours all of these, yet it also warns against repetition that leaves the heart untouched. The aim is that the remembrance *takes root* so that it shapes how we see, decide and act. One of Babaji's single divans is titled ‘Hirdhea Naam Vaseyo Ghar’, which, going by its title, takes up Naam coming to dwell in the heart.

**It is labour.** The closing Salok of Japji Sahib describes those who have meditated on Naam as having done hard work, *masakat ghāl*:

[[gurbani:jini-naam-dhiaya]]

This is an important corrective. Naam is not an escape from effort into a pleasant feeling. It is sustained work: returning attention again and again, especially when the mind runs elsewhere. The same line also says that ‘many are freed along with them’. Naam is never only private; it changes the people around the one who lives it.

**Trust in Naam.** A recent divan of Babaji's is titled ‘ਨਾਮ ਦਾ ਭਰੋਸਾ’, trust in Naam, alongside ‘ਮੁਕਤ ਕਿਵੇਂ ਹੋਈਏ’ (how do we become free) and ‘ਪੂਜਾ ਕਿਸ ਦੀ ਕਰੀਏ’ (whom should we worship). The pairing is telling: in Gurmat, Naam is both the practice and the thing trusted. The practice does not earn freedom like a wage; it opens a person to grace (*nadar*).

**Naam, Hukam and Shabad.** Gurbani often uses these words closely together. Naam is described as revealed through the Guru's Shabad, and living in Naam is living in harmony with the Hukam. They are not three separate subjects so much as three angles on one reality. Commentators differ on how precisely to distinguish them, and the site's theme pages treat them as connected rather than forcing a single definition.

## reflect

- When in your day is your mind most naturally quiet? Is there a place for remembrance there?
- What do you turn to first when you are anxious? What would it mean for Naam to become that first turn?
- Where does your practice feel like a habit, and where does it feel alive?

## practise

Choose one short, fixed moment you already have every day: waiting for a kettle, the walk to the car, the first minute after waking. For one week, use that moment for simran and nothing else. The aim is not a special experience. It is to make remembrance part of the shape of your day.
