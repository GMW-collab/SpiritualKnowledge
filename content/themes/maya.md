## discover

Anand Sahib gives one of Gurbani's clearest definitions of Maya. It does not describe a cosmic illusion. It describes something that happens in us.

[[gurbani:eh-maya]]

**Maya** (ਮਾਇਆ), in this sense, is whatever makes us forget the Divine: wealth, status, relationships and comforts when they take the place of the One. The result is *moh*, attachment, and *dūjā bhāu*, love of ‘the second’, of something other than the One.

## understand

**The problem is forgetting, not the world.** The world itself is not condemned. Gurbani describes creation as the Creator's own play, and the Gurus married, raised families, farmed, traded and built towns. What Gurbani diagnoses is a relationship to the world in which things become masters instead of gifts. A recent divan of Babaji's asks, in its title, ‘ਆਪਣਾ ਕੌਣ ਹੈ?’: who is truly ours?

**Vairag in the midst of life.** *Vairāg* means detachment or dispassion, and Babaji has given extensive katha on texts devoted to it. Gurbani's own image is precise:

[[gurbani:anjan-mahi]]

*Anjan* is soot or lamp-black; *niranjan* is the stainless. The yoga Gurbani accepts is not to flee the soot but to remain unstained within it. That is the Gurmat ideal of the householder (*grihast*) who lives fully in the world without being owned by it.

::: compare Bhartrihari's renunciation | Vairagya Shatak, attributed to the poet Bhartrihari
Babaji's 37-part katha on the Vairaag Shatak takes up one of the great poems of dispassion, the Sanskrit Vairagya Shatakam (the edition he read from is not recorded). Its verses expose the emptiness of pleasure, flattery, wealth and youth with unforgettable sharpness, and much of that diagnosis sits easily beside Gurbani. Where they part is the remedy. The Vairagya Shatak repeatedly praises withdrawal to the forest and the life of the renunciant. Gurbani rejects renunciation as the path: the Gurus criticised those who abandoned family and work and lived on others' labour. Gurmat keeps the Shatak's clear sight about the world, and replaces its exit with the householder's discipline.
:::

::: compare Advaita Vedanta | Vichar Sagar; Vedanta Darshan; Ashtavakra Gita
In Advaita Vedanta, *māyā* is the power by which the one reality, Brahman, appears as a world of many things; the world is called *mithyā*, neither simply real nor simply unreal. Gurbani shares the vocabulary and sometimes sounds close. Many Gurmat commentators, though, stress a difference: Gurbani speaks of creation as real and as the Divine's own expression, and locates Maya chiefly in our forgetting and attachment. The Nirmala tradition of learning, in which texts like Vichar Sagar were studied, often explained Gurbani using Vedantic terms; other Sikh scholars argue this blurs real differences. That debate is live, and this page does not settle it.
:::

## reflect

- What could you lose tomorrow that would most shake your sense of who you are?
- Is there something you own, or someone you love, that you hold in a way that makes you forget the Giver?
- What would it look like to remain ‘unstained in the soot’ in your own work?

## practise

Once each day, hold something precious to you, a cup, a photograph, a coin, and remember silently that it is given and will one day be returned. Then use it gratefully. The aim is not to love it less, but to love it without forgetting.
