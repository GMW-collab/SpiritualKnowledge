## discover

Gurbani has a word for the part of us that keeps saying *me* and *mine*: **haumai** (ਹਉਮੈ), literally ‘I, I’. It is not simply arrogance. It is the quiet, constant assumption that I am a separate centre around which everything else turns, and that what matters most is what happens to me.

Gurbani treats this as the root problem of human life. Yet it does not treat it as hopeless. Its most famous diagnosis comes with a promise attached.

[[gurbani:haumai-rog]]

## understand

**An illness, not a sin to be punished.** Calling haumai a *rog* (illness) changes how we relate to it. An illness is understood, treated and healed, not merely condemned. The same Salok goes on to say that if the Divine grants grace, a person acts according to the Guru's word, and in that way the illness goes. The cure is not the destruction of the self but its reorientation.

**How haumai works.** In the Asa ki Var, the saloks around this line describe a whole life lived inside haumai: coming and going, gaining and losing, praising and slandering, all measured from the point of view of ‘I’. The insight is that haumai is not one bad habit among many. It is the frame through which every experience is weighed. That is why Gurbani links it to the five thieves (kaam, krodh, lobh, moh and ahankar): each of them is haumai wanting something for itself.

[[gurbani:haumai-bujhai]]

**Understanding as the turning point.** The word *būjhai* matters. Haumai is not defeated by force of will, which is itself one more act of the ego. It is *understood*: seen for what it is. When it is seen, ‘the door comes into view’. Japji Sahib makes the same connection from the other side: whoever understands the Hukam no longer speaks from haumai. Haumai and Hukam are two ways of facing the same reality, from the narrow self or from the Order that holds everything.

**Pride and its small forms.** Haumai does not only show itself in grand pride. Gurbani and katha alike point to its ordinary forms: ingratitude (*akritghan*), resentment when we are not recognised, spiritual pride in our own practice. Babaji's recent divans name these directly in their titles and descriptions, such as ‘ਹੰਕਾਰ ਦੇ ਪੱਲੇ ਕੁੱਝ ਨਹੀਂ’ (pride has nothing to show for itself) and the fate of the ungrateful.

::: compare Buddhist teaching | Dhammapada; the Pali tradition generally
Buddhist teaching also locates suffering in clinging to a self, and Babaji has given katha on the Dhammapada. The resemblance is real: both see the grasping ‘I’ as the source of suffering. The difference is just as real. Classical Buddhist teaching (anattā) holds that no enduring self is found at all, and it does not speak of a Creator. Gurbani does not dissolve the self into nothing. It speaks of the self's true source, the Light within (*jot*), and of a living relationship with Waheguru through Naam and grace. In Gurbani, haumai is overcome by turning towards the One, not only by seeing through the self.
:::

## reflect

- When did you last feel slighted? What exactly was it that felt threatened?
- Which of your good qualities are you most proud of, and does that pride ever get in the way of them?
- What would change in one ordinary conversation today if you listened without waiting for your turn to be seen?

## practise

For one day, notice each time you silently make yourself the centre of a story: being overlooked, being right, being admired. Do not fight the thought. Name it quietly as *haumai* and return your attention to Waheguru, or to the person in front of you. Gurbani's claim is that seeing is already the start of the cure.
