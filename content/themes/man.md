## discover

Gurbani speaks to the mind (*man*, ਮਨ) more than to almost anything else. It addresses the mind directly, reasons with it, warns it and comforts it. And it tells the mind something surprising about itself.

[[gurbani:man-jot-saroop]]

The restless, wanting, worrying mind is not the enemy. It is the Light that has forgotten where it came from.

## understand

**Desire and restlessness.** The mind runs after *vāsnā* (deep-rooted desires) and *kāmnā* (wants), and throws up endless *phurne*, passing thoughts and impulses. Babaji's recent divans name these directly: ‘ਮਨ ਦੀਆਂ ਵਾਸ਼ਨਾਵਾਂ’ (the mind's desires) and ‘ਫੁਰਨੇ ਖਤਮ ਕਰਨੇ’ (ending the stream of impulses). His Man Budh Atam series includes talks titled ‘Kaamna Gyan Nu Rokda’, wanting obstructs knowledge, and ‘Mann Di Banavat’, the make-up of the mind.

**Purity is inward.** Gurbani is unsentimental about outward religion that leaves the mind unchanged:

[[gurbani:man-mailai]]

**The mind as the decisive ground.** Japji Sahib puts it in four words: *man jītai jag jīt*.

[[gurbani:man-jitai]]

‘Conquering’ here is not suppression by force. The context in Japji is the yogi's outward emblems, which the Guru reinterprets as inner qualities. The mind is won by being turned: towards Naam, the Shabad and the Guru, until it recognises its source.

::: compare Patanjali's yoga | Yoga Sutras (Yog Darshan), attributed to Patanjali
Babaji has given katha on the Yoga Sutras (Yog Darshan) and on the ‘Om Patanjali Yog Shastar’, including talks titled Samadhi, Sadhana, Sidhian and Moksh. Patanjali defines yoga as the stilling of the fluctuations of the mind (*chitta-vṛtti-nirodha*, Yoga Sutra 1.2) and lays out a disciplined path of ethics, posture, breath and concentration. Gurbani shares the concern with a restless mind and uses some of the same words, such as *samādhi*. It differs on the means and the goal. Gurbani is critical of yogic techniques pursued for their own sake or for powers (*siddhīs*), as Sidh Gosht shows, and it places Naam, the Shabad and grace, not technique, at the centre. Its goal is not a withdrawn stillness but *sahaj*, a natural poise within ordinary life.
:::

::: compare The Dhammapada | Dhammapada, from the Pali Buddhist canon
The Dhammapada opens by saying that mind precedes all things, and that a corrupted or a pure mind shapes everything that follows. Babaji's katha on the Dhammapada sits naturally beside Gurbani's teaching here. The difference lies in where the mind is finally turned: Buddhist teaching aims at the mind's own purification and the ending of craving; Gurbani turns the mind towards the Divine as its source and its rest.
:::

## reflect

- What desire is quietly running your days at the moment?
- When you sit quietly for a minute, what does your mind reach for first?
- What would it mean to treat your mind as Light that has forgotten itself, rather than as an enemy?

## practise

Sit for five minutes with simran. Each time a thought pulls you away, do not scold yourself: note where the mind went (a worry, a plan, a desire) and return. At the end, write down the three places it went most. Over a week they will show you your *vāsnā* more honestly than any theory.
