## discover

The very first pauri of Japji Sahib asks the question at the heart of a spiritual life: how do we become true, and how is the wall of falsehood between us and the Divine brought down? Guru Nanak Dev Ji's answer is not a ritual or a technique. It is a way of walking.

[[gurbani:hukam-rajai]]

**Hukam** (ਹੁਕਮ) is the Divine Order: the will by which everything comes into being, is sustained and changes. To walk in the Hukam is to live in harmony with that Order rather than against it.

## understand

**Everything is already within it.** The second pauri of Japji opens by saying that forms are created by Hukam, life is given by Hukam, and greatness, joy and suffering come by Hukam. Then it closes the circle:

[[gurbani:hukmai-andar]]

This is one of Gurbani's most important connections. No one stands outside the Hukam; the only question is whether we *understand* it. And the sign of understanding is that haumai falls silent. Resistance to the Hukam and the voice of the ego are the same thing seen from two sides.

**Beyond full description.** Japji is also clear that Hukam cannot be fully described: ‘the Hukam cannot be told’ is how the second pauri begins. Gurbani asks us to *bujhna* the Hukam, a verb that means to understand or to grasp, as one solves a riddle, rather than to define it. Sikh commentators differ on how far Hukam can be known through reason. Most agree it is recognised through Naam and the Guru's Shabad more than through argument.

**Acceptance is not passivity.** Hukam is sometimes heard as fatalism: whatever happens is God's will, so nothing need be done. Gurmat does not teach that. The same Gurus who sang of the sweetness of the Divine will (*bhāṇā mīṭhā*) also stood against injustice, built institutions, worked, served and, when necessary, fought. Living in Hukam means acting fully while letting go of the demand that outcomes obey me. Babaji's recent katha puts it plainly in its title: ‘ਹੁਕਮ ਮੰਨਣਾਂ ਬਹੁਤ ਔਖਾ’, obeying the Hukam is very hard. It is a discipline, not a resignation.

**Hukam and karma.** Gurbani speaks both of Hukam and of the consequences of our own actions (see the theme on conduct). These are not presented as rival systems. Our actions bear fruit within the Order, and grace (*nadar*) can still transform what our actions alone would produce. How these fit together is one of the deepest questions in Gurmat, and it is honest to admit that commentators explain it in different ways.

## reflect

- Where in your life are you still arguing with what has already happened?
- Can you name one situation where acceptance would make you *more* able to act well, not less?
- How do you tell the difference between surrendering to the Hukam and simply avoiding effort?

## practise

At the end of each day this week, recall one thing that did not go as you wanted. Say inwardly the phrase often heard in Sikh homes, ‘ਤੇਰਾ ਭਾਣਾ ਮੀਠਾ’ (your will is sweet), and then ask honestly whether anything is still yours to do about it. Do that part. Leave the rest.
