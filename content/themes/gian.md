## discover

Gurbani invites a search inward. Bhagat Pipa Ji's line, included by Guru Arjan Dev Ji in the Granth, puts the whole cosmos inside the seeker.

[[gurbani:brahmande-pinde]]

*Giān* (ਗਿਆਨ) is spiritual knowledge: not information, but seeing what is so. *Ātmā* (ਆਤਮਾ) is the self in its deepest sense. Babaji's katha gives unusual space to these questions. His series include Man Budh Atam (talks titled ‘Aatma Ki Hai’, what is the self, and ‘Gyan Ki Hai’, what is knowledge) and long katha on classical texts of Indian philosophy.

## understand

**Knowledge that frees, and knowledge that binds.** Gurbani honours *giān* as the Guru's gift that dispels the darkness of ignorance.

[[gurbani:gian-anjan]]

But Gurbani is just as clear that learning alone can become one more possession of the ego:

[[gurbani:path-pario]]

Babaji gave a divan in January 2010 on this very shabad (‘Paath Pariyo Aur Ved Bichariyo’). Reading, reflecting and spiritual technique did not break the hold of the five thieves; they tightened the knot of self-conceit. Gurmat *giān* is knowledge that humbles.

**Why a Gurmat teacher reads Vedanta.** Babaji's archive includes katha on the Vedanta Darshan, Vichar Sagar, Vichar Mala and the Ashtavakra Gita. This is part of a long tradition: Sikh scholars of the Nirmala and Udasi traditions, and sants associated with them, studied and expounded Indian philosophical texts and read them in the light of Gurbani. Listening to that katha, it helps to know where these texts and Gurbani meet and where they do not.

::: compare Advaita Vedanta | Vedanta Darshan (the Brahma Sutras); Vichar Sagar; Ashtavakra Gita
**Where they meet.** Advaita Vedanta teaches that the deepest self (*ātman*) is not other than the one reality (*brahman*), and that bondage comes from ignorance of this. Gurbani also speaks of the One within all, of the Light in every heart, and of ignorance (*agiān*) as darkness.

**Where they part.** Advaita holds that liberation comes through knowledge (*jñāna*) of that identity, and often treats devotion as a preparatory stage. Gurbani places Naam, the Guru's Shabad, love and grace (*nadar*) at the centre, and keeps the relationship of devotee and Beloved even in the highest states. Advaita commonly describes the world as *mithyā*; Gurbani speaks of creation as real and as the Divine's own play. The Ashtavakra Gita's radical claim that the self is already free and needs no practice sits uneasily beside Gurbani's insistence on discipline, seva and Naam.

**Where it is contested.** Historically, Nirmala scholarship often expressed Gurbani in Vedantic terms; many modern Sikh scholars argue this overstates the similarities. Listeners will hear both emphases in different katha. This site treats Gurbani as the standard against which the comparison is made.
:::

## reflect

- What have you learned about spiritual life that has made you humbler? What has made you prouder?
- When you say ‘I’, what exactly do you mean: the body, the mind, a history, something deeper?
- Is there knowledge you already have that you are not yet living?

## practise

Choose one line of Gurbani you understand intellectually. For a week, ask each evening: where did I live this today, and where did I only know it? Write one sentence each night. Knowledge in Gurmat is tested in the living of it.
