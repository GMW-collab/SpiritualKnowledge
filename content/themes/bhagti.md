## discover

Gurbani's invitation to love is also a warning about its cost.

[[gurbani:jau-tau-prem]]

**Bhagti** (ਭਗਤੀ) is devotion; **prem** (ਪ੍ਰੇਮ) is love. In Gurmat they are not a soft alternative to the hard work of spiritual life. They are its heart. Gurbani is full of the voice of the soul-bride longing for her Beloved, and much of the Granth is the bani of bhagats, devotees from many backgrounds whose love the Gurus honoured.

## understand

**Head on the palm.** To come into the lane of love ‘with your head on your palm’ is to bring nothing to bargain with. The image became a lived reality in Sikh history, from the Panj Pyare at Anandpur onwards. Babaji's lineage traces itself to Bhai Daya Singh Ji, the first to answer that call.

**Birha, the pain of separation.** The Granth holds two Barah Maha compositions: Guru Arjan Dev Ji's in Raag Majh and Guru Nanak Dev Ji's in Raag Tukhari. Babaji's Baramaha series has ten recordings; their titles do not say which composition each one covers. Both follow the soul through the twelve months of the year, each season colouring its longing. In Gurbani the ache of separation (*birha*) is not a failure of faith. It is often the sign that love is alive.

**The language of the beloved.** Babaji's katha on the Persian ghazals of Bhai Nand Lal Ji (‘Goya’), the poet at Guru Gobind Singh Ji's court, opens another register of devotion: the wine, the tavern and the beloved's face of the Persian mystical tradition, turned towards the Guru.

**Devotion and daily life.** In his Man Budh Atam series, Babaji's talks include ‘Bhagti Te Prem’ (devotion and love), ‘Bhagti De Marg’ (the paths of devotion) and ‘Bhagti Te Khaana’ (devotion and food), the last a reminder that devotion is shaped by ordinary disciplines like what and how we eat. His recent divan titled ‘ਭਜਨ ਅਤੇ ਭੋਜਨ ਸਹਿਜ ਨਾਲ’ (worship and eating, both with ease) makes the same link.

::: compare Bhakti in the wider Indian tradition | The bhakti movements; bani of the Bhagats in the Granth
The Granth includes the bani of bhagats such as Kabir, Namdev, Ravidas and Farid, and Gurbani shares the bhakti movement's insistence that love, not caste or ritual, is what matters to the Divine. It differs from many bhakti traditions in rejecting the worship of images and incarnations (*avtārs*): the Beloved of Gurbani is the formless One (*nirankār*), even when spoken of in the most intimate, personal language.
:::

## reflect

- When have you felt longing for the Divine? What did it feel like, and what did you do with it?
- What would you be unwilling to give up for love?
- Does your devotion bargain? What are you hoping to receive?

## practise

Listen to one month of the Barah Maha with its katha, ideally the month you are now in. Then, through the rest of that month, let its image return to you once a day. Let the season outside become a reminder of the longing inside.
